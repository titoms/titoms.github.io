If you ask ten agencies how much an MVP costs, you will get ten different numbers, and most of them can be technically true.

That is not always dishonesty. It is because the question is incomplete. Cost is not a property of the word "MVP." It is a property of the scope behind it, and most founders lock a budget before that scope really exists.

Here is the realistic range, what moves the number and why the estimate you get in week one is often wrong.

## The realistic MVP cost range in 2025

Across freelancers, agencies and small in-house teams, most MVPs land somewhere between **$10,000 and $150,000**. That range is wide because it describes very different products.

| MVP type | Typical range | Typical timeline | Team model |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Landing page plus waitlist or one simple workflow | $5,000-$15,000 | 2-4 weeks | Solo freelancer |
| Standard MVP with auth, one core feature and a basic dashboard | $15,000-$50,000 | 6-12 weeks | Freelancer or small agency |
| Mid-complexity MVP with roles, integrations or payments | $50,000-$100,000 | 3-5 months | Agency or small in-house team |
| Complex MVP with compliance, AI features or multi-platform work | $100,000-$250,000+ | 5+ months | Larger team or specialized roles |

Rates vary as much as scope. Freelancers and offshore teams can be much cheaper than Western Europe or North America agencies. Specialized senior teams can cost several times more than a solo builder.

The same idea can therefore have a 3x or 4x cost difference depending on who builds it and how much is really included.

## What actually drives the price

Five variables explain most of the gap between a $15K MVP and a $150K MVP.

## Scope and complexity

A login form, one core workflow and email notifications is not the same project as multi-role permissions, real-time collaboration, billing, admin tools or an AI pipeline.

Every extra user type, rule and failure path adds work that does not show up in a one-line feature list.

## Team model and location

A solo freelancer, a small studio, an agency and an in-house team do not price risk the same way.

A freelancer can move quickly when the scope is narrow. An agency may cost more but can bring design, QA, project management and delivery process. An in-house team is rarely the cheapest way to validate a first version, but it can make sense once the product is strategic enough.

## Stack and platform

A single web app is cheaper than web plus iOS plus Android from day one.

A content-heavy MVP might only need Astro, a form and analytics. A SaaS product with accounts, billing and private dashboards needs backend logic, persistence, authentication, emails and monitoring.

If you are still unsure which stack fits the first version, read the [React, TypeScript and Node.js MVP stack guide](/blog/react-typescript-node-mvp-stack/) before asking for quotes.

## Design depth

A functional UI built from an existing design system is cheaper than custom UX research, wireframes, brand direction and high-polish visual design before the first line of code.

That does not mean design is optional. It means design depth should match the validation goal. Most early MVPs need clarity, usability and trust more than a full brand system.

## Speed

Compressing a twelve-week build into six weeks is not just "faster." It usually means more people working in parallel, more coordination and more decision pressure.

Speed has a price, and that price often shows up as management overhead or rework.

## The hidden costs in many MVP quotes

The number in the proposal is rarely the number you actually pay.

Common missing costs include:

- Discovery and scope clarification.
- Project management and coordination.
- Hosting, infrastructure and third-party APIs.
- Authentication, payment and email provider fees.
- Legal and compliance work.
- QA and post-launch bug fixing.
- Maintenance after launch.

If a quote does not mention at least a few of these, it is not a complete budget. It is a starting number.

A realistic plan usually includes a 20% contingency buffer. That is not pessimism. It is respect for the fact that software projects discover details while they are being built.

## Why early MVP estimates are often wrong

Most estimates are wrong because the thing being estimated is not defined yet.

A founder says "user dashboard" or "payment integration," but those are categories, not features.

For a dashboard:

- Which users can access it?
- What data appears first?
- What happens when data is empty?
- Who can edit what?
- Is export required?
- Does it need audit history?

For payments:

- One-time payment or subscription?
- Trial or no trial?
- Failed payment retry?
- Invoice handling?
- Role change after cancellation?
- Tax handling?

Until those decisions are made, the estimate is a guess with a currency symbol attached.

## How to scope so the budget holds

The goal is not to make the product small forever. The goal is to make the first version small enough to test.

A useful scoping process looks like this:

1. Define the target user and the first successful outcome.
2. List the minimum workflow that proves that outcome.
3. Separate launch requirements from version-two ideas.
4. Decide which integrations are required now.
5. Write failure paths before implementation starts.
6. Add a budget buffer before signing.
7. Freeze scope for the first build phase.

The most important artifact is not a huge backlog. It is a feature-freeze list: what ships in version one, what is deferred and what would break the budget if added mid-build.

## Freelancer, agency or in-house?

For a first MVP, the best team model depends on risk.

A freelancer is often the best fit when:

- The scope is narrow.
- The founder can make decisions quickly.
- The product is a web app or prototype.
- The budget is limited.
- Speed matters more than process.

An agency can make sense when:

- You need design, backend and QA at the same time.
- The product has many workflows.
- Stakeholders need formal process.
- Reliability matters more than the lowest cost.

An in-house team makes sense later, when the product is validated enough that the codebase becomes a strategic asset.

## The real answer

There is no honest single answer to "how much does an MVP cost in 2025?"

A useful answer is narrower: a well-scoped web app MVP with one clear user group, one core workflow, a defined stack and a 20% buffer often lands between **$15,000 and $50,000** for many early founders.

When an estimate misses that range, the reason is usually visible in hindsight: the scope was not fixed, the edge cases were not discussed, or the MVP quietly became a bigger product.

Before you ask someone to build the MVP, make the first version explicit. That is where the budget is won or lost.

## FAQ

### Can I build an MVP for under $10,000?

Yes, if the scope is genuinely minimal: one core workflow, no complex roles, no heavy integrations and a lean freelancer or no-code approach.

### Is a fixed-price quote safer than time and materials?

Fixed price is safer only when the scope is frozen. If requirements are still moving, a capped time-and-materials approach after discovery is usually more honest.

### Why did my MVP cost more than the first quote?

Usually because discovery was skipped, features were vague or scope creep accumulated quietly. The first quote priced an incomplete version of the product.

### Should I hire a freelancer or an agency for my first MVP?

A freelancer can be better for a small single-feature MVP. An agency makes more sense when you need several roles coordinated at once.
